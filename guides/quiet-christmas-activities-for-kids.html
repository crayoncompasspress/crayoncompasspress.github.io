Guides for grown-ups

Quiet Christmas activities for kids ages 3 to 6: at home, in the car, on a plane and at a restaurant

The best quiet Christmas activities for kids ages 3 to 6 are short, simple and screen-free: coloring and small activity pages, stickers, painter's tape, pipe cleaners, and talking games that need nothing at all. At home or on the road, have the next activity ready before the current one runs out, and save one new thing for the hardest part of the day. Below is what to pack, what works at home, in the car, on a plane and at a restaurant, and a calm plan for Christmas Eve.

Why short, simple activities work well

A tired, excited child in a loud place often gives up on a big blank coloring page within a minute or two. Pages with one small job and a clear finish, like a short maze or a "draw the hat on the snowman" game, are easier to start and easier to finish, and many children find that more manageable.

So for trips, take two or three single pages folded into quarters, not a whole book. They fit a coat pocket and come out one at a time. Keep the full activity book at home, where a few pages a day can last all of December.

Many children this age move on from an activity after a few minutes; others stay with it much longer. Never interrupt a child who is happily busy. Just have the next thing ready for when interest fades: about 3 or 4 activities for a restaurant meal, and 6 to 8 for a long flight or drive.

Quiet activity bag checklist

Choose by your child, not just by age. Supervise anything with wire, small pieces or stickers, and leave out anything your child might put in their mouth.

A zip pouch or small tote that lives by the door all of December saves a lot of last-minute hunting. Folded pages fit a pouch; a full-size activity book needs a tote or backpack.

  • Crayons, not markers. Short or triangle crayons roll less, and there are no caps to lose under a seat.
  • A few activity pages. For 3-year-olds: coloring, tracing and wide, easy mazes. For ages 4 to 6: mazes and numbered dot-to-dots too. The blank backs make good drawing paper.
  • Stickers. Puffy or reusable stickers are easiest for small fingers. Before you leave, peel away the background border around flat stickers so a 3-year-old can lift them alone.
  • Painter's tape for making roads, shapes and tic-tac-toe grids on a tray table or a window at home. It peels off clean.
  • Pipe cleaners to bend into candy canes, stars and rings. Fold the sharp wire tips inward first, and stay close while they are in use.
  • One die in a small clear plastic snap-shut box or clean plastic spice jar, for roll-and-draw games. Children shake it and peek, and it cannot roll away.
  • One or two small new things, such as a mini sticky-note pad, a sheet of window gel clings or a little animal figure. Unwrapping is half the fun, so hand them out one at a time when things get tough.

Small parts: a die, small figures, pom-poms, small window gel clings and broken crayon bits can be choking hazards. Leave them out for babies, toddlers and any child who still puts things in their mouth.

Quiet Christmas activities at home (while you cook or wrap)

  • Ornament sorting: sort one-piece unbreakable ornaments (take off any metal hooks or caps first) or large pom-poms by color into a muffin tin (ages 3 and up who no longer put things in their mouths).
  • Window gel clings: decorate a low window, then rearrange (ages 3 and up; keep clings on glass, since the colored gel can mark walls and fabric).
  • Coloring with an audiobook or a favorite Christmas song playing.
  • One or two activity-book pages at the kitchen table, so you are close by.

Screen-free Christmas car games

Talking games work in any seat and need no light, no table and no supplies. In a moving car, skip hard clipboards and heavy books, which can fly forward if you brake hard. A soft folder or notebook is fine to lean on.

  • Christmas light count. Everyone counts houses with lights. Younger children can just shout "lights!"
  • I spy colors. "I spy something red" is easier for 3-year-olds than letters. Older children can try "I spy something that starts with the 'sss' sound."
  • Slow-motion words. Say a word slowly and let your child guess it. With 3-year-olds, start with word parts: "snow... man" or "rein... deer." Ages 4 to 6 can try single sounds, stretched without stopping (this is called blending): "sssuuunnn" (sun), "mmmaaannn" (man), "fffuuunnn" (fun). It is a real pre-reading skill too: see our guide to teaching blending.
  • Story chain. You say one line ("A little reindeer lost her bell..."), your child adds the next, and you take turns.
  • Christmas songs with actions: clap, tap knees, wiggle fingers, or sing them in a squeaky voice.

If your child feels unwell looking down at a page in the car, skip coloring and books on the road and stick to talking and looking-out-the-window games.

Quiet activities for a Christmas flight

A tray table is a small, perfect desk. Before boarding, let your child walk, stretch and move at the gate, and keep the quiet bag closed until you are in the air.

  • Painter's tape on the tray: make a road and "drive" two fingers along it, or tape a simple shape to trace with a finger. Peel the tape off before you land.
  • Mazes, and dot-to-dots for ages 4 and up: short, with a clear finish line, and easy to pause when the snack cart arrives.
  • Sticker scenes: draw a quick Christmas tree outline on the back of a page and let your child add sticker "ornaments."
  • Window gel clings and mini sticky notes: flat, silent and easy to peel off.

Always follow the crew's instructions. During takeoff and landing, tray tables must be stowed, so plan lap-only things then: a drink or snack (swallowing can help with ear pressure), a small new thing to unwrap, or a talking game. For plane surprises, use a small pouch or reusable gift bag instead of wrapping each one: security staff may need to look inside your bags, and unwrapped items are quicker to hand over in a tight seat. Small, flat items are easiest. Anything that rolls ends up under the seat in front.

Quiet activities for kids at a restaurant

The first hard part is the wait between ordering and the food arriving. Bring out one or two pages and two crayons, then put everything away before hot plates reach the table. Save one last thing for after your child has finished eating, while the grown-ups finish.

  • Roll and draw a snowman (ages 4 to 6, or 3-year-olds with your help): shake the boxed die, then draw the matching part: 1 bottom snowball, 2 middle snowball, 3 head, 4 hat, 5 stick arms, 6 face. If a part comes before its snowball, just draw it floating, or roll again. It is one of our two free printable pages, and it also works on a napkin.
  • Draw it together: you draw one part of a picture, your child adds the next.
  • Spot it: find three red things, something round, and something that starts with the hard "k" sound, like cup, cake or candle.
  • Tic-tac-toe on a napkin, for 5- and 6-year-olds.

For toddlers, and any 3-year-old who still puts small things in their mouth, keep it simple: chunky crayons, puffy stickers, and matching crayons to things of the same color on the table. Leave the die at home.

A calm Christmas Eve routine

Christmas Eve is often the most excited, least sleepy night of the year. A simple, quiet routine helps.

  1. Afternoon: one hands-on job, like decorating a paper placemat to go under the cookie plate, or writing (or drawing) a note for the reindeer.
  2. Early evening: a 10- to 15-minute walk to look at the neighbors' lights uses up some of the jitters.
  3. After dinner: a slow, still activity like a maze, a dot-to-dot, or counting the ornaments on the tree together (eyes only, hands behind backs if the ornaments are glass).
  4. Before bed: bath, pajamas, lights low, and a cozy picture book read aloud. Many families wrap the book and pajamas as a "Christmas Eve box" to open after dinner.

Try to keep the last hour before bed screen-free and quiet. It is fine if sleep comes late tonight. Calm is the goal, not a perfect bedtime.

Questions parents ask

What are good quiet Christmas activities for a 4-year-old?

Good quiet Christmas activities for a 4-year-old include short mazes and easy dot-to-dots, coloring a Christmas picture, sticker scenes, bending pipe cleaners into candy canes and stars (fold the wire tips in first), a roll-a-die-and-draw-a-snowman game, and counting the ornaments on the tree. Have the next activity ready for when interest fades, but don't interrupt a child who is happily busy.

How do I keep a 3-year-old busy on a long car ride without screens?

Use talking games that need no supplies: count houses with Christmas lights, play "I spy something red," sing songs with hand actions and take turns adding lines to a story. Add puffy stickers and a small new toy for the longest stretch, and skip hard clipboards or heavy books in a moving car. If your child feels unwell looking at pages in the car, skip coloring and books.

What should I put in a restaurant activity bag for kids?

Pack a few chunky crayons (not markers), two or three folded activity pages such as a small maze or a roll-and-draw game, and a small sheet of puffy stickers. For ages 3 and up who no longer put things in their mouths, add one die in a small closed box. Bring out one item at a time while you wait for food, put things away when the plates arrive, and save one for after your child has eaten.

What can kids do on Christmas Eve to calm down before bed?

Plan one hands-on job in the afternoon, such as drawing a note for the reindeer or decorating a placemat for the cookie plate. Take a short walk to see the lights in the early evening, do a quiet activity like a maze after dinner, then a bath, pajamas, low lights and a picture book read aloud. Keeping the last hour before bed screen-free and quiet helps.

What are quiet Christmas activities that need no supplies?

Counting houses with Christmas lights, "I spy something red," a story chain where you take turns adding a line, and spotting shapes or colors around you are quiet Christmas activities that need no supplies. They work well in the car and in waiting rooms because children need no table, toys or screens.
